South Punjab cooking — and the knife it needs.

Multan's culinary tradition is one of the most distinctive in Pakistan. Five centuries of Sufi hospitality, agricultural abundance and the region's hot climate have shaped a kitchen culture where cooking is generous, slow and serious. Guests arrive unannounced and leave well fed — this is the social contract of the Multani household.

The kitchen in Cantt, Bosan Road and Shah Rukn-e-Alam Colony centres on large-format dishes: whole leg mutton for sajji or a slow roast, deep daal mash cooked with fresh turmeric root and whole garlic, large-volume pulao for gathering weekends. The 8-inch chef knife breaks down the meat at the joint, chops the aromatics and handles the volume without slowing down. A stamped bazaar knife blunts at this pace. A forged, full-tang blade does not.

Multan's most celebrated export is sohan halwa — the city's defining sweet. Making it properly requires careful slicing of almonds, pistachios and dried apricots into even pieces. The 3.5-inch paring knife, used in a controlled pull-cut against a flat board, produces uniform slices without bruising the nuts. The mirror-polished blade clears clean of sticky sweet residue.

Mango season runs May through September and turns Multani households into processing centres. Chaunsa, Sarauli and Langra mangoes arrive by the crate from nearby groves. Halving, peeling and slicing a hundred mangoes for a gathering requires a blade that does not fatigue the hand. The 8-inch knife, balanced at its full tang and weighted deliberately toward the heel, handles a mango session without wrist strain. In winter, the same kitchen makes large batches of lime and mango achar that take days of daily cutting. A blade used every morning needs to hold its edge for weeks — ours does.
